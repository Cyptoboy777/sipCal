What a SIP is, and what it is not

A systematic investment plan (SIP) lets you invest a fixed amount in a mutual fund at regular intervals, usually monthly. Each instalment buys units at that day’s net asset value (NAV). When prices are low your money buys more units; when prices are high it buys fewer. Over a long period this spreads your entry price across many market levels instead of staking everything on one day.

A SIP is a way of investing, not a product with a fixed return. The fund you choose decides the risk. An equity fund can fall sharply in a bad year; a debt fund usually moves less but is not risk-free. No SIP calculator, including this one, can know what your fund will earn. It answers a narrower question: if your money grew at a steady rate, what would your instalments add up to?

The SIP formula

Future value = P × [((1 + i)n − 1) ÷ i] × (1 + i) P is your monthly investment. i is the monthly rate, the expected annual return divided by 12 and by 100. n is the number of months. The final (1 + i) reflects that each instalment is invested at the start of the month.

With a yearly step-up, a starting lump sum or an inflation adjustment, the calculator runs the same logic month by month: add the instalment, apply a month of growth, and raise the instalment by your step-up percentage at the end of each year. Today’s-money value is the final amount divided by (1 + inflation) for each year invested.

A worked example: $400 a month at 12% for 15 years

If you invest $400 every month for 15 years, you put in $72,000 in total. At a steady 12% a year that grows to about $201,830, which means roughly $129,830 comes from returns. Time is doing most of the work, and the table below shows how much longer horizons add:

Step-up SIP: raising your instalment as income grows

Most people earn more each year, so a fixed instalment shrinks in relative terms. A step-up raises the SIP by a set percentage every year. Open Add a lump sum, yearly step-up or inflation above to try it. With a 10% yearly step-up, the same starting $400 grows to $347,354 over 15 years instead of $201,830, on total contributions of $152,508 against $72,000. Our step-up guide compares several percentages.

Inflation: what the number is worth in today’s money

A large figure in the future buys less than the same figure today. If inflation averages 6% a year, $201,830 in 15 years has the buying power of about $84,217 in today’s money. Setting the inflation field above shows this as a separate line, so you can judge a goal in terms you can feel. If you want to buy what costs $100,000 today, plan around what that same thing will cost then, not today’s price.

Starting with a lump sum

If you also invest a one-time amount at the start, the lump sum compounds for the full period. Adding $20,000 up front to the same monthly SIP lifts the 15-year value from $201,830 to $321,746.

SIP versus lump sum

A lump sum is invested all at once; a SIP builds the position gradually. If markets rise steadily, the lump sum wins because more money was working for longer. If markets fall soon after you invest, the SIP wins because later instalments buy cheaper units. Nobody can know in advance. In practice the choice is often decided by cash flow: most people invest from monthly income, which makes a SIP the natural fit, and it also builds a habit that does not depend on guessing market timing. “Rupee cost averaging” lowers the risk of a poorly timed entry, but it does not protect you from a falling market, and it does not guarantee a profit.

What this calculator leaves out

  • Costs. A fund’s expense ratio is already reflected in its NAV, so use a return net of costs. Some funds charge an exit load if you redeem early.
  • Taxes. Gains from mutual funds can be taxable, and the rules differ between equity and debt funds and have changed in the past. Check the current rules or ask a tax professional.
  • Irregular investing. Missed or changed instalments will change the result.
Mutual fund investments are subject to market risks. Past performance does not guarantee future results. The return you enter is an assumption, not a forecast, and this calculator does not recommend any fund.

Frequently asked questions

How is SIP return calculated here?

We assume your money grows at a steady annual rate, converted to a monthly rate, with each instalment invested at the start of the month. The formula is P × [((1 + i)^n − 1) ÷ i] × (1 + i). Real funds fluctuate, so the actual outcome will differ.

What return should I assume?

There is no single right answer, and no one can promise a return. Try several rates, such as a cautious figure and an optimistic one, to see a range. Use lower rates for debt-oriented funds and be conservative for short horizons.

Is the SIP return guaranteed?

No. SIP investments in mutual funds are market-linked and can lose value. The calculator shows what would happen at the rate you type in, which is an assumption and not a forecast.

What is a step-up SIP?

It is a SIP whose instalment rises by a fixed percentage each year, for example 10%. It lets your investment keep pace with income growth and can lift the final value substantially over long periods.

Can I stop or change a SIP?

Generally yes. Most funds let you pause, change or cancel a SIP with a notice period set by the fund house or platform. Check the terms of your fund and any exit load on units you redeem.

Why show the value in today&rsquo;s money?

Because prices rise over time. The inflation-adjusted figure estimates what the future amount could buy in terms of today’s prices, which is a better basis for judging whether a goal is on track.

Does this include taxes and fund charges?

No. Use an expected return that is already net of the fund’s expense ratio, and check the current tax rules for your type of fund separately.
