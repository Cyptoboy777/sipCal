What an EMI actually is

An equated monthly instalment (EMI) is the fixed amount you pay your lender every month until a loan is cleared. It is called “equated” because the payment stays the same, even though what it is made of keeps changing. Each EMI is split into two parts: interest on the balance you still owe, and repayment of the loan itself (the principal). In the early years most of the instalment is interest. As the balance shrinks, the interest part shrinks too, and more of the same payment goes to principal.

That shift is the single most useful thing to understand about a loan. It explains why prepaying early saves far more than prepaying late, and why a longer tenure makes a loan look cheap monthly while costing a great deal more overall.

The EMI formula

EMI = P × r × (1 + r)n ÷ [(1 + r)n − 1] P is the loan amount. r is the monthly interest rate, which is the annual rate divided by 12 and by 100. n is the number of monthly instalments. If the rate is 0%, the EMI is simply P ÷ n.

This calculator uses exactly that formula, then builds the month-by-month schedule by charging interest on the opening balance each month and moving the rest of the EMI against principal. The schedule always ends at zero, which is how we check the arithmetic: the principal repaid across all months must add back up to the amount you borrowed.

A worked example: $200,000 at 8.5% for 20 years

Take a home loan of $200,000 at 8.5% a year over 20 years (240 instalments). The monthly rate is 8.5 ÷ 12 ÷ 100 = 0.007083. Putting that into the formula gives an EMI of $1,736.

Over the full term you would pay $416,555 in total, of which $216,555 is interest. That is 52.0% of everything you pay, and it is more than the $200,000 you borrowed. Here is how the same EMI changes character over time:

A single percentage point can be worth thousands over a long loan. If your loan has a floating rate linked to an external benchmark, your EMI or tenure can change when the benchmark moves, so treat any single figure as a snapshot, not a promise.

Reducing balance versus flat rate

Most home, car and personal loans from banks are quoted on a reducing balance, meaning interest is charged only on what you still owe. Some dealers and lenders quote a flat rate, where interest is calculated on the full original amount for the entire term. A flat rate of 8% is not the same as a reducing rate of 8%. Because you are repaying principal every month, the equivalent reducing-balance rate on a flat-rate loan is typically close to double the quoted figure. If a lender quotes a flat rate, ask for the annual percentage rate on a reducing balance, then enter that here to see the true cost.

What an EMI figure does not include

  • Fees. Processing fees, documentation charges, and any insurance bundled with the loan are not part of the EMI formula. Add them to your own cost comparison.
  • Rate resets. On floating-rate loans, a change in the benchmark changes your EMI or your remaining tenure.
  • Pre-EMI periods. For an under-construction property, you may pay interest only on the amount disbursed so far until the loan is fully disbursed.
  • Day-count differences. Banks may compute interest daily or on specific dates, so the final cents on your actual schedule can differ slightly from any calculator, including this one.
Use your lender’s schedule for the final word. This tool is built for planning and comparison. The sanction letter and repayment schedule from your lender are the binding figures.

Ways to reduce what a loan costs

Open the “Add a monthly prepayment” section above and try an extra $200 a month on the $200,000 example. The loan clears in 15 yrs 7 mos instead of 20 yrs, and you save $55,570 in interest. Raise the extra payment to $400 and the loan ends in 12 yrs 11 mos with $87,149 saved. Other levers worth checking:

  • Compare rates, not just EMIs. Ask each lender for the rate, the fees, and whether the rate is fixed or floating, then run each offer through the calculator.
  • Look after your credit profile. Many lenders offer better rates to borrowers with strong credit histories, so pay existing dues on time and avoid unnecessary new credit before applying.
  • Check prepayment terms. Rules differ by country: some jurisdictions bar prepayment penalties on certain loans, while others allow them, and fixed-rate loans often carry charges. Read your sanction letter.
  • Consider a balance transfer carefully. A lower rate elsewhere helps only if the savings outweigh the new processing fee and any charges on the old loan.

Frequently asked questions

How is EMI calculated?

EMI = P × r × (1 + r)^n ÷ [(1 + r)^n − 1], where P is the loan amount, r is the monthly interest rate (annual rate ÷ 12 ÷ 100) and n is the number of months. The calculator above applies this formula and then builds the full month-by-month schedule.

Why is so much of my early EMI interest?

Interest is charged on the outstanding balance, which is highest at the start. With a $200,000 loan at 8.5% for 20 years, 81.6% of the first instalment is interest. By the final year it is a tiny fraction.

Does a longer tenure save money?

It lowers your monthly EMI but increases the total interest you pay, often by a very large amount. A longer tenure helps cash flow; a shorter one helps total cost. The tenure table above shows the trade-off for a real example.

What is the difference between flat and reducing-balance interest?

Reducing-balance interest is charged only on the amount still owed, which is how most bank loans work. Flat-rate interest is charged on the original amount for the whole term, so the effective cost is much higher than the quoted number suggests.

Can I reduce my EMI or my tenure after prepaying?

Usually yes, but it depends on the lender. Cutting the tenure normally saves more interest than cutting the EMI. This calculator models the tenure-reduction approach: your EMI stays the same and the loan ends sooner.

Is this the same figure my bank will show?

It should be very close for a standard reducing-balance loan, but small differences are normal because banks apply their own day-count and rounding rules. Fees and insurance are not included here. Your lender’s schedule is the final reference.

Are my numbers stored or sent anywhere?

No. The calculation runs entirely in your browser. Nothing you type into the calculator is sent to our servers.
