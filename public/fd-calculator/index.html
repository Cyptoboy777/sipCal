How a fixed deposit earns interest

A fixed deposit (FD) lets you lock a lump sum with a bank or finance company for a fixed period at a stated rate. There are two common ways to take the interest:

  • Cumulative (reinvestment) FD. Interest is added to the deposit and compounds until maturity, when you receive everything at once. This is what the calculator above models.
  • Non-cumulative (payout) FD. Interest is paid out monthly, quarterly or yearly, so the deposit itself does not grow. You earn simple interest on the original amount.

Many banks compound deposits quarterly, but this varies by country and institution, so choose the compounding that matches your deposit in the calculator.

The FD maturity formula

Maturity = P × (1 + r ÷ n)n × t P is the deposit. r is the annual rate as a decimal. n is the number of times interest is added each year (4 for quarterly). t is the term in years.

A worked example: $20,000 at 7% for 5 years

A $20,000 cumulative FD at 7% with quarterly compounding matures at $28,296 after five years. The interest earned is $8,296, and the effective yearly yield is 7.19%, a little higher than the quoted 7% because interest is added four times a year. How the compounding choice changes the same deposit:

Longer deposits earn disproportionately more because interest is compounding for longer. But they also lock your money away, and interest rates change, so a long term can leave you stuck at a rate that later looks low. A “ladder” of deposits with staggered maturity dates is a common way to balance access and return.

Things to check before you book an FD

  • Tax. FD interest is generally taxable as part of your income at your slab rate, and banks may deduct tax at source (TDS) once interest crosses a threshold. Thresholds and rules change and differ for senior citizens, so check the current rules.
  • Premature withdrawal. Breaking an FD early usually reduces the rate and may carry a penalty. Check the terms.
  • Deposit insurance. Bank deposits are usually covered by a national deposit-insurance scheme (for example the FDIC in the US, the FSCS in the UK, or the DICGC in India) up to a limit per depositor per bank. Check the current limit in your country. Spreading large sums across institutions can reduce concentration risk.
  • Real returns. If inflation is close to your FD rate, your purchasing power grows slowly or not at all after tax. Compare the after-tax, after-inflation return with your goal.
  • Company deposits. Corporate FDs may offer higher rates but are not covered by DICGC insurance and carry credit risk.

How to use this calculator

  1. Enter your deposit amount, the bank’s quoted annual rate and the deposit period. You can enter a fraction of a year, such as 1.5 for 18 months.
  2. Pick how often interest is added. For most bank cumulative FDs that is quarterly.
  3. Read the maturity amount, interest earned and effective yield. Compare two banks by changing only the rate.
  4. Use the year-by-year table to see how the balance builds.
Your bank’s certificate is final. Banks may compound on specific dates or use day counts that differ slightly from this model, so the exact maturity value can differ by a few units of money.

Frequently asked questions

How is FD maturity calculated?

For a cumulative FD, maturity = P × (1 + r ÷ n)^(n × t), where n is the number of compounding periods per year. Many banks compound quarterly, so n is 4.

What is the difference between cumulative and non-cumulative FDs?

A cumulative FD reinvests interest and pays everything at maturity. A non-cumulative FD pays interest out at regular intervals and returns only the principal at maturity, so it does not compound.

Why is the effective yield higher than the quoted rate?

Because interest is added more than once a year, later interest is earned on earlier interest. A 7% quoted rate compounded quarterly is an effective yield of about 7.19%.

Is FD interest taxable?

Generally yes, as income at your applicable slab rate, and TDS may apply above certain thresholds. Rules change and vary by age, so check the latest rules from the Income Tax Department or a tax professional.

Is my money safe in an FD?

Bank deposits are insured by the DICGC up to a stated limit per depositor per bank. Amounts above the limit, and deposits with non-bank companies, are not covered in the same way.

Can I use this for a recurring deposit (RD)?

Not exactly. An RD takes a deposit every month, and banks compound it quarterly. For a close approximation of regular deposits, try the compound interest calculator with a monthly deposit.
