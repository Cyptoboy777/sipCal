The rule of 72

A mental shortcut that turns any interest rate into a doubling time, in about two seconds.

Reviewed 15 November 2026 · 4 min read

The rule

To estimate how many years it takes for money to double at a given annual rate of return, divide 72 by the rate. At 8%, it takes about 72 ÷ 8 = 9 years. At 12%, about 6 years. At 6%, about 12 years. It works the same way for debt that compounds against you: a loan or card balance growing at 18% roughly doubles in 4 years if left alone.

Where it comes from

With yearly compounding, money doubles when (1 + r)t = 2. Solving for t gives t = ln(2) ÷ ln(1 + r). For small rates, ln(1 + r) is close to r, so t is about 0.693 ÷ r, or 69.3 ÷ the rate in percent. Seventy-two is used instead of 69.3 because it is easier to divide (it has many factors: 2, 3, 4, 6, 8, 9, 12) and it corrects for the fact that the approximation is a little off at ordinary rates.

The rule is very close between roughly 6% and 12%, which is where most savings and investment assumptions live. It drifts at the extremes: it slightly overstates the doubling time at low rates, and understates it at high ones. For rates above about 20%, use 70 or the exact formula in the compound interest calculator, which shows the exact doubling time for any rate.

Useful ways to apply it

  • Fixed deposits. A deposit at 7.2% doubles in about 10 years. Check the real figure for your rate in the FD calculator.
  • Inflation. At 6% inflation, prices double in about 12 years, so what costs ₹1,00,000 today could cost ₹2,00,000 then. Prices that double in twelve years halve the buying power of money that does not grow.
  • Debt. A balance growing at 36% a year doubles in about 2 years, which is why credit card debt left to compound is so dangerous.
  • Sanity checks. If a product promises to double your money in 3 years, the rule says it implies about 24% a year, which is a reason to ask hard questions.
  • Fees. A cost of 1% a year on a 8% return changes the doubling time from 9 years to about 10.3 years.

What the rule cannot do

It says nothing about regular contributions. If you add money every month, as in a SIP, money does not double on a simple schedule, because new money keeps arriving. It also assumes a steady return, while real returns vary, and it ignores tax and fees. Use it for a quick intuition, and use the calculators for numbers you plan around.

The one-line summary. Divide 72 by the rate to get the doubling time in years. Good between about 6% and 12%; use the exact formula elsewhere.

Frequently asked questions

Why 72 and not 70 or 69?

69.3 is the most accurate for continuous compounding, and 70 for low rates. Seventy-two works well at the 6 to 12% range and divides evenly by many common rates, so it is easier to use in your head.

Can I use the rule of 72 for a SIP?

Not directly. It works for a single amount that grows undisturbed. With monthly contributions, use the SIP calculator.

Does the rule work for inflation?

Yes. Divide 72 by the inflation rate to estimate how long it takes prices to double, or the buying power of money to halve.
