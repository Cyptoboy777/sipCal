SIP step-up explained

A fixed SIP quietly shrinks as your income grows. A step-up fixes that by raising the instalment on a schedule you choose.

Reviewed 15 November 2026 · 5 min read

What a step-up SIP is

A step-up (or top-up) SIP increases your monthly instalment by a fixed percentage or amount every year. If you start at $400 with a 10% yearly step-up, you invest $400 a month in year one, $440 in year two, $484 in year three, and so on. The idea is that your investing keeps pace with your income instead of staying frozen at what felt right the year you started.

What it does over 20 years

Here is a $400 monthly SIP at a steady 12% a year for 20 years, with four different step-ups:

Yearly step-upYou investFinal valueReturns
No step-up$96,000$399,659$303,659
5% a year$158,717$549,505$390,788
10% a year$274,920$795,549$520,629
15% a year$491,729$1,210,238$718,508

A 10% step-up more than doubles the final value compared with a flat SIP ($795,549 against $399,659), but you also contribute more: $274,920 against $96,000. In year 20 the monthly instalment has grown to about $2,446. A fair way to see the benefit is to ask what flat SIP would give the same result. You would need to invest about $796 a month from day one, which is 2.0 times the starting amount. A step-up lets you reach the same place while starting small.

Why a step-up works well in practice

  • It follows your income. Raises and promotions make a bigger SIP affordable, and automating the increase means you do not need to decide each year.
  • It keeps your investing in step with inflation. A fixed $400 buys less each year. A step-up at least matches rising prices, and ideally exceeds them.
  • It removes a decision. The habit that is hardest to keep is the one you must remember. An automatic increase makes the better behaviour the default.

Choosing a step-up percentage

  1. Start from your income growth. If your pay rises about 8% a year, a step-up of 5 to 8% leaves your take-home growing too.
  2. Be honest about cash flow. A step-up you cannot sustain tends to end in a paused SIP. A smaller one that you keep is worth more than a larger one you cancel.
  3. Try several values. Use the yearly step-up field in the SIP calculator to see how 5%, 10% and 15% change your goal.
  4. Review every year or two. Raise it after a big pay rise or once an EMI ends, and lower it if your circumstances change.

What to watch for

The projections assume a steady return, which real markets do not deliver, and a step-up increases your exposure to a bad market late in the journey, when more money is invested. If your goal is close, shift towards less volatile holdings rather than relying on a high assumed return. Also check whether your platform supports step-up on the fund you chose; most do, but the options and the frequency differ.

Illustration only. The figures assume a constant 12% return. Mutual fund investments are subject to market risks, and actual outcomes can be well above or below these numbers.

Frequently asked questions

Is a step-up SIP better than a flat SIP?

It usually produces a larger corpus because you contribute more over time, and it keeps your investing in step with your income. It is better only if you can sustain the higher instalments.

How much step-up should I choose?

A sensible starting point is a figure close to your expected income growth, often between 5% and 10%. Choose a rate you will actually keep.

Can I change or stop a step-up?

Generally yes. Most platforms let you modify or cancel a SIP or its step-up. Check with the platform or fund house for the process.
