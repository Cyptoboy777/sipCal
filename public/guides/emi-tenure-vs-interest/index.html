Shorter or longer loan: what tenure really costs

The tenure you choose decides your monthly EMI and, much more quietly, how many lakhs of interest you pay. Here are the real numbers.

Reviewed 15 November 2026 · 6 min read

The same loan at six different tenures

Take a loan of ₹50,00,000 at 8.5%, the same amount and the same rate, and change only the number of years:

Two things stand out. First, the EMI falls quickly at first and then barely at all: stretching from 25 to 30 years lowers the EMI by only ₹1,816 a month, yet adds ₹17,62,036 of interest. Second, at 30 years you pay more in interest (₹88,40,443) than the ₹50,00,000 you borrowed.

The cost of the last few years

The extra years at the end of a long loan are the most expensive per rupee of EMI saved. Compare going from 15 to 20 years, and from 20 to 30:

  • 15 to 20 years: the EMI drops by ₹5,846 a month, and total interest rises by ₹15,51,223.
  • 20 to 30 years: the EMI drops by ₹4,945 a month, and total interest rises by ₹34,26,564.

Each additional year buys you a little breathing room today and charges you a lot for it later.

A middle path: choose a long tenure, pay like a short one

You do not have to pick one extreme. Many borrowers take a longer tenure for a lower required EMI, which protects them in a lean month, and then pay extra when they can. On the ₹50,00,000 loan over 25 years, adding ₹10,000 a month on top of the EMI cuts the real tenure to 14 yrs 5 mos, and saves ₹33,93,862. The difference from a short loan is that the extra payment is optional. Check that your lender allows part-payments without a penalty; for floating-rate loans to individuals, RBI rules generally bar prepayment charges, but fixed-rate loans may carry them.

How much EMI can you afford?

There is no universal safe figure, but lenders often look at the share of your monthly income that goes to fixed obligations, and commonly cap it somewhere around 40 to 50%. For your own planning, a stricter test is useful: after paying every EMI, would you still cover essentials, an emergency fund contribution and your regular investing, even if your income dipped? If the answer is only “yes, barely”, a longer tenure is a reasonable safety margin.

  1. Write down your take-home income and your fixed monthly costs, including all existing EMIs.
  2. Subtract them to see what a new EMI could comfortably be, then leave a buffer.
  3. Try tenures in the EMI calculator until the EMI fits inside that figure.
  4. If the only tenure that fits is very long, consider whether a smaller loan or a bigger down payment is the better fix.

Other things that change the picture

  • Interest rate changes. On a floating loan, a rate rise can lengthen your tenure or raise your EMI. A long tenure leaves more time for rates to move.
  • Your income trajectory. If you expect your income to grow, a shorter tenure becomes easier over time, and a longer one can be shortened later by prepaying.
  • Tax benefits. Some loans carry deductions on interest or principal under the tax rules in force. They do change, so check the current rules before relying on them, and do not take a bigger loan just for a deduction.
The short version. Choose the shortest tenure you can pay without strain, keep the option to prepay, and run the numbers for two or three tenures before you decide.

Frequently asked questions

Is a 30-year loan a bad idea?

Not necessarily. It gives the lowest EMI and the most flexibility, which can suit a tight budget. It also costs the most in interest, so many borrowers pair a long tenure with regular prepayments.

How much interest does a ₹50.00 L loan cost over 20 years at 8.5%?

About ₹54,13,879, or 108.3% of the amount borrowed, on a monthly EMI of ₹43,391.

Can I change my tenure later?

Often yes. Lenders usually allow part-prepayments, and some allow you to revisit the EMI or tenure. Terms vary, so confirm with your lender before you sign.
