Prepay a loan or invest the money?

Both routes use the same cash. The only real question is whether your investment will beat your loan rate, after tax and after risk.

Reviewed 15 November 2026 · 7 min read

Prepaying is a guaranteed return equal to your loan rate

Every rupee you prepay stops charging interest at your loan’s rate for the rest of the term. If your loan costs 8.5%, prepaying earns you a certain 8.5% before tax, with no market risk. That is the number any investment has to beat to be worth choosing instead, and it has to beat it by enough to compensate for the risk that it may not.

The effect is big when done early. On a ₹50,00,000 loan at 8.5% for 20 years, an extra ₹5,000 every month ends the loan in 15 yrs 7 mos instead of 20 yrs, and saves ₹13,89,250 in interest.

A like-for-like comparison

To compare the routes fairly, both must use identical cash every month. We set it up like this on the same loan:

  • Route A: prepay. Pay the EMI plus ₹5,000 extra. The loan ends in 15 yrs 7 mos. After that, the money you were paying (EMI plus ₹5,000) is invested instead, until month 240.
  • Route B: invest. Pay only the EMI on the original schedule and invest ₹5,000 every month for all 240 months.

Either way you pay out ₹48,391 each month. Here is what you hold at the end of 20 years, with the loan fully repaid in both cases, if the investment earns a steady return, before tax:

At 8.5%, the break-even, the routes are equal, which is the point of the exercise: prepaying behaves like an investment that earns your loan rate. Investment returns above that favour Route B, and returns below it favour Route A. Real returns are not steady, so the table shows where the lines cross, not what will happen.

What the table leaves out

  • Risk. Route A’s return is certain. Route B’s is not, and a year or two of poor returns early on can leave you behind even if the long-run average looks fine.
  • Tax on both sides. Investment gains may be taxed. Loan interest may carry a deduction under the rules in force. Both change the break-even, in different directions, so check the current rules.
  • Liquidity. A prepayment cannot be taken back out. An investment can usually be sold in an emergency. This is a real advantage of investing, and a reason to hold an emergency fund before doing either.
  • Behaviour. Some people find a prepayment is the more reliable habit, because the money is spent on something concrete. Others do better with automated investing.

A practical order of operations

  1. Emergency fund first. Several months of expenses in an accessible account, so a surprise does not force you to break an investment or miss an EMI.
  2. Expensive debt next. Credit card balances and personal loans usually cost far more than any investment can reliably earn. Clear these before anything else.
  3. Then compare your loan rate with realistic after-tax returns. The higher the loan rate, the stronger the case for prepaying.
  4. Consider splitting. Many people prepay part and invest part, which reduces the regret of choosing wrong.

This is not advice to pick either route. It shows how to frame the choice. Your tax position, risk tolerance and goals decide which side wins for you.

Frequently asked questions

Is it better to prepay a home loan or invest in mutual funds?

It depends on whether your after-tax investment return, adjusted for risk, is likely to exceed your loan rate. Prepaying gives a certain return equal to the loan rate; investing offers the chance of more, with the risk of less.

Which saves more interest, prepaying early or late?

Early. Interest is charged on the outstanding balance, which is largest at the start, so a rupee prepaid in year two removes many more future interest charges than a rupee prepaid in year eighteen.

Do I pay a penalty for prepaying?

For floating-rate loans to individuals, RBI rules generally prohibit prepayment charges. Fixed-rate loans and loans to non-individual borrowers may have them. Check your sanction letter.
